The pitch for putting land records on a blockchain is easy to make and mostly
wrong. It goes: land disputes happen because records get altered, blockchains
cannot be altered, therefore blockchain fixes land disputes.

Every step of that is true except the conclusion. Working on a registry for a
state government taught me why, and the lesson turned out to be about state
management generally rather than about land.

## Two guarantees that look like one

An immutable ledger gives you exactly one thing: once a fact is written, you
can prove it has not changed, and you can prove when it was written.

It gives you nothing at all about whether the fact was true when it was
written.

This is the oracle problem, and it is not solved by a better consensus
algorithm. If the clerk enters the wrong owner, you now have a permanent,
tamper-evident, cryptographically signed record of the wrong owner. The system
worked perfectly and produced a worse outcome than a paper ledger, because a
paper ledger can be corrected with a pen and an explanation.

## So why build it at all

Because "hard to change quietly" turns out to be worth a great deal on its own.

The disputes that a registry system can actually address are not the ones where
the original entry was wrong. They are the ones where the record was correct
and then changed — a transfer recorded twice, a parcel that appears in two
chains of title, an alteration nobody can date.

Those are detection problems. And detection is precisely what an append-only
log with a verifiable history gives you. Not prevention — detection.

Once you accept that framing, the design changes. You stop trying to make the
system the source of truth and start trying to make it the *audit* of truth:

- Records are never edited. A correction is a new entry that supersedes an old
  one, and both remain readable.
- Every entry carries who submitted it and under what authority.
- The chain of title for a parcel is reconstructed by replaying entries, not
  by reading a current-state row.

## Which is just event sourcing

At which point an experienced backend engineer will point out that this is
event sourcing, and they are right. Append-only log, derived read models,
corrections as compensating events. None of it requires a blockchain.

That is a fair challenge and worth answering honestly, because it decides
whether the chain earns its place.

The honest answer is that a database gives you the append-only log, and a
blockchain gives you one additional property: *the operator of the database
cannot rewrite history without it being obvious to someone who does not trust
them.*

For most systems that property is worth nothing, because you already trust the
operator — it is your own company, and the auditors have access. For a public
registry, where the party running the database is also a party to the disputes
it adjudicates, that property is the entire point.

That is the test I now apply. Not "would immutability be nice", but: **is there
a party who needs to verify this history without trusting whoever stores it?**
If no, use Postgres. It is faster, cheaper, and you can fix things.

## What actually shipped

The system that went in looks unremarkable on a diagram. A React application
officials use to register, search and verify parcels. An API and database
holding the working state, because officials need sub-second search across
millions of records and no chain does that. Solidity contracts holding record
state and enforcing the rules a transfer must satisfy.

Most of the engineering was not the contracts. It was reconciliation: the
registry had decades of existing records in inconsistent formats, and any
system that cannot answer questions about those records is a system nobody in
the department can use.

That is the part nobody puts in the pitch deck, and it is where the project
either works or does not.

## The generalisation

The thing I took from it applies well outside land records.

When you are deciding where a piece of state lives, the useful question is not
how important the data is. It is **who needs to be able to disagree with you
about it later**. Data nobody will contest can live wherever it is cheapest.
Data that will be contested needs a history, and the history needs to be
verifiable by the party doing the contesting.

Everything else — the chain, the database, the file — is an implementation
detail of that one requirement.
